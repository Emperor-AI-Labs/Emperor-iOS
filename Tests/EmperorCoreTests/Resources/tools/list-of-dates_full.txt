Prepare a LIST OF DATES for an Indian litigation matter from the case record. This is not a summary. In Indian practice the List of Dates is a formal part of the paperbook: the Hon'ble Court reads it before it reads the pleadings, and the other side goes through it line by line. One wrong or unsupported date discredits the whole document. Precision outranks coverage, and an admitted gap outranks a confident guess.

Deliverable: Limitation Chronology.
Forum: NCLT. Match that forum's convention — in the Supreme Court the Synopsis and List of Dates is required by the Supreme Court Rules, 2013 and the Practice Directions issued thereunder, and page references are to the paperbook; in the High Courts a synopsis and list of dates precedes a writ petition, but whether it is required at all and how pages are referred to differ between the Delhi, Bombay, Madras and other High Courts, so read the Court's name off the cause-title in the record and state which Court's convention you have assumed, so that counsel can correct it in one line instead of discovering it at the filing counter; before the NCLT, NCLAT and other tribunals the same chronology is filed alongside the statement on limitation.
Prepared on behalf of: the Petitioner. Refer to the parties by their status in the present proceedings (Petitioner, Respondent, Appellant, Complainant), giving the full name at first mention. The narrative may be told from this party's standpoint, but never at the cost of an accurate date and never by dropping an event that is on the record.
Counsel's instructions / focus:
The 2019 invoices only.
This governs the DEPTH of treatment, not what goes in. Every dated event on the record stays in the table; the events counsel has pointed to get fuller Event and Note text and closer citation. Restrict the chronology to a period or a transaction only where counsel has expressly asked for that restriction, and where you do, say so on the face of the document ("Restricted chronology: events from DD.MM.YYYY to DD.MM.YYYY only") so nobody mistakes it for the full List.

WHERE THE DATES MUST COME FROM
Go through the attached documents front to back rather than skimming; read specific page ranges where you need to and search the record for dates you expect to exist but have not yet located. Harvest dates from where they hide, not only from the narrative: the date line and subscription of each letter, postal and courier receipts, speed-post tracking, dishonour memos, endorsements, seals and stamps, acknowledgements, the attestation date on an affidavit, the vakalatnama, order sheets and daily proceedings, the date of pronouncement on a judgment, the registration endorsement on a deed, invoice and payment dates, and statutory filing acknowledgements.
If nothing has been attached and no facts appear in the instructions above, do not invent a matter: say plainly that no record was supplied, then output the correct blank court format (the table with its headings) with a checklist of the documents and the classes of dated events counsel should supply for a matter of this kind. Invent no party, no date and no case number.
If facts have been pasted instead of documents attached, build the chronology from those facts but mark every entry as resting on counsel's instructions and not yet verified against a document on record.

SAY WHAT YOU ACTUALLY READ
A List of Dates that is silently partial is worse than no List at all, because it still reads as complete and it still gets filed. A paperbook can run to thousands of pages and you may not be able to cover all of it. Therefore, in the notes block of a filed version and at the head of an internal chronology, state in plain terms: (a) the documents and the page ranges you actually read; (b) the documents that were attached but which you did not read, or could not read; and (c) where the record could not be covered end to end, that the List is PROVISIONAL and confined to the material covered, and what remains to be gone through. Never present partial coverage as complete, and never pad the table to make it look complete.

LENGTH
A preferred output length is appended to this request. It governs the SYNOPSIS and the notes only. It does not govern the table: no dated event on the record may be dropped from the table, and no entry may be compressed to the point where the act it records is no longer identifiable, in order to meet a length preference. Where the record is genuinely too large to list in full, say so, list what you covered and mark the List provisional — never truncate the table without notice.

THE SEVEN RULES THAT MAKE A LIST OF DATES LEGALLY CORRECT

1. NAME THE ACT, NOT THE DOCUMENT. Issue, despatch, service, receipt, refusal and reply are separate events with separate legal consequences, and limitation almost always runs from one specific one. Never collapse them into a single row to save space. Where the record shows more than one, each gets its own row and the Event column must state which act occurred:
   - notice issued / despatched / served / received / refused / returned unclaimed;
   - order reserved / pronounced / signed / uploaded, and certified copy applied for / prepared / delivered (those three decide the Section 12 Limitation Act exclusion);
   - suit or petition presented / registered / returned for compliance / re-filed;
   - cheque drawn / presented / dishonoured / memo received / statutory notice under Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 despatched and served / fifteen-day period expired / complaint filed — these rows exist so that counsel can test the three statutory intervals set out under LIMITATION below, so do not merge them;
   - agreement executed / registered / possession delivered / consideration paid;
   - notice invoking arbitration under Section 21 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 issued and received / tribunal constituted / statement of claim filed / award made and received / petition under Section 34 filed.
   Where the record shows only one half of such a pair (a notice dated 05.03.2019 with no proof of service), say so in that row instead of assuming the other half happened.

2. SEPARATE WHAT IS EVIDENCED FROM WHAT IS MERELY ASSERTED. A date proved by a dated instrument on the record is a different animal from a date averred in a pleading. Classify every entry:
   D — evidenced by a document on the record (the dated instrument itself, an endorsement, a receipt, an order);
   P — asserted in a pleading, affidavit, notice or written submission only, with no supporting document on record;
   I — inferred, computed or approximate.
   This is precisely what opposing counsel will attack, so it must be visible to counsel even where it is not printed in the filed version.

3. NORMALISE THE DATE, PRESERVE WHAT THE DOCUMENT SAID. The same date appears in Indian documents as 05.03.2019, 5th March 2019, 05/03/2019, 5-3-19 and March 5, 2019. Render every date in the output as DD.MM.YYYY.
   - Where the document's own rendering differs materially from the normalised form — a spelled-out date, a two-digit year, a date written in words in a deed — record the source form in the notes ("the page reads 'the Fifth day of March, Two Thousand Nineteen'"), because opposing counsel will check the List against the page.
   - Where a numeric date is genuinely ambiguous between DD/MM and MM/DD (03/05/2019), do not silently pick a reading. Resolve it only from context — the surrounding sequence, a spelled-out date for the same event elsewhere, the document's own convention — and state the basis. If it cannot be resolved, keep the document's own form, mark it AMBIGUOUS and say which reading changes what.
   - Where the year is given in two digits (5-3-19), do not assume the century. Fix it only from the record — the age of the instrument, the stamp or registration particulars, the surrounding sequence — and say what fixed it. In succession, title and property matters 1919 and 2019 are both live; where the record cannot settle it, give both readings and mark it AMBIGUOUS.
   - Where only a month, quarter or financial year is given, render it as given (03.2019, FY 2019-20), place it after the last certain date within that period and mark "(day not stated)". Do not manufacture a day.
   - Compute relative expressions ("within two weeks thereafter", "the following Monday") only where the anchor date is certain, and show the computation in the entry; otherwise keep the words.
   - Where the document carries an obvious internal error, such as a letter dated after the reply it answers, reproduce what it says and flag the discrepancy. Do not correct it silently.

4. NEVER RECONSTRUCT A DIGIT. Indian paperbooks are scans of scans. Dates come through mangled by OCR (l5.l2.20l8, O5.O3.2Ol9, 0S.03.20I9), sit under a round stamp or a seal, are cut off at the margin, or are written in hand on an endorsement. The temptation is to repair the character into the plausible date — that is fabrication, and here it lands in the Date column of a document that goes to the Bench. Where a date cannot be read with confidence: reproduce the characters exactly as they appear, mark the entry ILLEGIBLE with the document name and page, state the readings the marks could bear if any are open, and name what must be done — the page to be re-scanned, the certified copy or the original to be obtained, the deponent to be asked. Do the same for a date obscured by a stamp and for a handwritten endorsement you cannot read. An ILLEGIBLE row that names the page is useful; a clean row carrying a guessed digit is a liability.

5. PIN-CITE EVERY ENTRY. No entry without a source. Cite the document by the name it carries in the record together with the page, paragraph or annexure — in a Supreme Court or High Court paperbook the form is "Annexure P-1 @ p. 45", "Impugned order dated 12.04.2021 @ p. 112", "Writ Petition, para 7"; in a trial-court record it is the exhibit form, "Ex. P-3, p. 45". Where the attached paperbook is paginated, use the paperbook page, because that is the page the Hon'ble Court will turn to; otherwise cite the document's own page. If you cannot cite an entry, do not write it.

6. CONFLICTS ARE OUTPUT, NOT NOISE. Where two sources give different dates for the same event, do not choose. Show the event once carrying both dates and both citations, mark it CONFLICT, and say which source is the stronger — a contemporaneous document ordinarily beats a later pleading — without suppressing the other.

7. SILENCE IS OUTPUT TOO. Where the record does not establish something, say so in terms ("the record does not show when the notice was served") and name the document that would establish it. A stated gap is useful to counsel; a filled gap is a liability. Never supply a date, figure, account number or party name that is not in the record.

STRUCTURE OF THE DOCUMENT
Order strictly by date; where two events share a date, order them as the record shows they occurred.

If the deliverable is "Synopsis and List of Dates (for filing)" or "List of Dates only (for filing)":
- Open with a single heading, SYNOPSIS AND LIST OF DATES (or LIST OF DATES), and under it one line identifying the matter to the extent the record supports it (parties and case number). Nothing else above it.
- SYNOPSIS (only when it has been asked for): continuous narrative prose in the register of the court, with no bullet points and no sub-headings, running from the origin of the dispute to the relief now sought and closing with the questions of law arising if the record supports them. Carry page references in brackets in the paperbook style, "(at p. 45)". Keep the length proportionate to the record.
- LIST OF DATES: an HTML table with three columns, "Date" | "Event" | "Page". One row per event. The Event column is a single crisp sentence in the past tense naming the actor and the act. No commentary, no argument and no D/P/I tags inside this table — the filed version must read as a court document.
- Then a horizontal rule and a block headed NOTES FOR COUNSEL — NOT FOR FILING (delete before filing), carrying only: (a) the coverage statement required above — what was read, what was not, and whether the List is provisional; (b) the High Court or forum convention assumed, where the record left it open; (c) the entries resting on assertion (P) or inference (I), with what would evidence each; (d) conflicting dates with both sources; (e) ambiguous, partial or ILLEGIBLE dates, the reading adopted and the source form where it differs materially from the normalised form; (f) documents referred to in the record but not on the record; (g) the limitation analysis below.

If the deliverable is "Working Chronology (internal)":
- Open with the coverage statement, then a short block headed CONFLICTS, GAPS AND LIMITATION EXPOSURE — no more than a dozen lines, each one sentence with its pin-cite, carrying only the conflicting dates, the missing links and the intervals that are close to or beyond a prescribed period. This is what counsel opens the note to find, so it goes above the table, not below it.
- Then an HTML table with five columns, "Date" | "Event" | "Source (document, page / para)" | "Basis (D/P/I)" | "Note". Include everything dated, including what looks trivial. The Note column carries the working observations counsel actually needs: a contradiction with another document, an admission, an unexplained gap in time, an act done after limitation had arguably run, a signature date that does not match the body.
- Then, under separate headings and in full: CONFLICTING DATES; GAPS IN THE RECORD (documents referred to but not produced, and periods for which there is no material at all); LIMITATION INTERVALS; OPEN QUESTIONS FOR COUNSEL.

If the deliverable is "Limitation Chronology":
- The dated events bearing on limitation only, in the same five-column table, followed by a computation table with columns "Relief / proceeding" | "Governing Article or provision (Limitation Act, 1963, or the special statute; state 'Limitation Act not applicable — writ' where it is a writ)" | "Time begins to run from (event and date)" | "Prescribed period" | "Expiry" | "Filed on (or 'not yet filed')" | "Days elapsed vs. prescribed period" | "Source". The days column carries arithmetic only — the number of days and the direction — never a conclusion that the proceeding is in time or out of time.

If something other than the above was asked for, follow the closest of these formats and adapt it.

LIMITATION, WHICHEVER FORMAT WAS CHOSEN
Compute from the dated events in the table, show the arithmetic and state the assumption behind each computation. Where a date the computation needs is not on the record, say the interval cannot be computed and name the document that would supply it; do not assume it.
Set out the intervals anyone will check first:
- cause of action to institution of the suit, naming the governing Article of the Limitation Act, 1963 where the nature of the claim identifies it;
- the impugned order to the appeal or revision;
- Section 138 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — three intervals, which is why the drawn / presented / dishonoured / memo received rows are kept apart: presentation of the cheque within the period of its validity under proviso (a); the statutory notice given within thirty days of receipt of information from the bank of the dishonour under proviso (b); and the complaint filed within one month of the date on which the cause of action arose under clause (c) of the proviso, that is on the expiry of fifteen days from service of the notice, as required by Section 142(1)(b);
- the notice invoking arbitration under Section 21 to the application under Section 11;
- receipt of the award to a petition under Section 34 of the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 — three months, and the further thirty days which is an outer limit and not an ordinary condonation: Section 5 of the Limitation Act, 1963 does not apply to it, so delay beyond the three months and thirty days cannot be condoned at all. Where a request under Section 33 was made to the arbitral tribunal, time runs from the date that request was disposed of and not from receipt of the award;
- appeals to the NCLAT — under Section 61(2) of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016, thirty days from the order with a further fifteen days condonable on sufficient cause, the period running from the pronouncement of the order and not from receipt of a copy; under Section 421(3) of the Companies Act, 2013, forty-five days from the date on which a copy of the order is made available to the aggrieved person, with a further forty-five days condonable. State the certified-copy position as a separate line in either case: the date the certified copy was applied for and the date it was made ready or delivered, and whether the Section 12(2) exclusion can be computed on this record at all.
WRITS. Do not name an Article of the Limitation Act, 1963 and do not state a prescribed period for a petition under Article 226 or Article 32 — the Act does not apply to such petitions. Address delay and laches instead: the date of the impugned action, the date on which the Petitioner came to know of it and the material on the record that establishes that knowledge, the explanation for the delay that appears on the record and whether it is on affidavit, any representation made and the date it was decided or rejected, and any period fixed by the special statute or the service rules under which the impugned action was taken.
EXCLUSIONS. State which the record supports and which it does not. Section 12(2), for the time taken in obtaining the certified copy, applies only where the record shows both the date of application and the date the copy was ready, so if it does not, say the exclusion cannot be computed and name the document needed; the same discipline applies to Section 14, to an acknowledgement in writing under Section 18 and to part payment under Section 19.
COVID EXCLUSION. Where a computed period touches the window from 15.03.2020 to 28.02.2022, apply the Hon'ble Supreme Court's order dated 10.01.2022 in In Re: Cognizance for Extension of Limitation in both its halves and show both computations: that period stands excluded in computing limitation; and where the balance period remaining as on 01.03.2022 is less than ninety days, the party has ninety days from 01.03.2022, that ninety-day floor being the half that usually decides whether a filing is in time.
Present the computation and what turns on it. Report the arithmetic, not the verdict: do not announce a conclusion that a claim is barred or saved — that is for counsel to settle.
